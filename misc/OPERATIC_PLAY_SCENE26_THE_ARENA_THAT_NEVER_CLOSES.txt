OPERATIC PLAY, SCENE 26: THE ARENA THAT NEVER CLOSES
(sigma-ground, the night of 2026-11-02 into 2026-11-03)

The planets had run out of future. Reality for them was DE442, JPL's latest fit, and it ended in 2024; the next one would come in some years, and
until then every comparison of our N-body prediction with the planets was a comparison with the past. The Captain had drawn the conclusion that
afternoon: the live arena is the small bodies. Telescopes measure asteroids every night, the Minor Planet Center publishes the raw positions the same
week, and JPL's own solutions move with them. If the prediction was to go on being tested, it would be tested there, daily, against positions nobody
had seen when the prediction was made.

So the session built a scoreboard. Thirty-two bodies: the thirty heaviest asteroids in DE440, Eros (the one body already tested this way, in C24),
and Apophis. For each, the rules were fixed before any number was read: our arm would be the C24 engine from a start state that never moves, so the
prediction ages in public; JPL's arm would be Horizons' own topocentric answer at the exact instants of the observations, none of our pipeline in it;
and a body with nothing new in the sky would be written down as a row of zeros, never guessed at. The ledger got the expected sizes first: both arms
inside the telescopes' own 0.3-0.5 arcsec, ours within 0.02 arcsec of JPL's on day one, and a guess, from the two brightest bodies in an older fetch,
that at most ten of the thirty heavies would have twenty observations this year.

The first probe request went to Horizons alone, for two Eros observations from a Chilean station, and came back 0.1 arcsec from what the telescope
had recorded. Then the dry run on Eros found the first bug: the propagation flew off to 10^37 km. C24's Eros vectors are in km per second and the
asteroid group's are in km per day, and the scorer had read both the same way. The units line is now parsed and a speed that is not a solar-system
speed is refused. The second dry run gave Eros's ten monthly windows of 2026 at 0.08 to 0.19 arcsec, and the real run began at 03:43 UTC under the
watchdog.

Thirty-seven minutes later the board had 154 rows and 5,706 observations on it. Ours and JPL's agreed to 0.0015 arcsec at worst, on Eros at a lead
of six and a half years, and to 0.0002 arcsec on the heavies. The guess about coverage was wrong in the good direction: twenty-five of the thirty
heavies had twenty or more observations in 2026, Bertha alone 519. The surveys skip the giants that saturate their detectors and track everything
fainter every month. P3 is recorded as failed, and the arena is richer than the entry expected. One honest hole: Horizons answered every request for station C40,
the Kuban observatory that made 558 of Eros's 1,160 observations this year, with a 502 error page. Sent again after the run, eighty-seven instants
got the same error and two instants got an ephemeris: the server chokes on long requests for that site. Those 558 observations have our residual and
no JPL one and sit outside both arms on the first day's rows; the scorer now splits a refused request in halves so the next day does not lose them.

While the board was filling, a message came from the session upstairs: the Captain wanted the other thing gone, the line that said every arm starts
from JPL's state. A prediction from a borrowed start shows parity of dynamics; it is not an ephemeris of our own. So the session wrote the fit:
six numbers at 2020-01-01, corrected by least squares to five years of Eros observations with our propagation as the model, finite-difference partials,
equal weights, outliers out at three sigma. The synthetic test caught the second bug of the night before the real data saw it: C24's velocity step of
five metres per day was so small that the integrator's own noise was a tenth of the partial; at a hundred metres per day the partials agree with their
half-step to a twentieth of a percent, and the synthetic orbit came back inside three sigma in two iterations.

The real fit took three iterations and six minutes. Post-fit scatter 0.369 arcsec, nineteen of 1,563 observations rejected, and the fitted state 33 km
and 2.4 millimetres per second from JPL's, every component within two scaled sigma. Then the three curves on the 4,435 observations that came after:
from our fitted state, from JPL's state, and JPL's own. For four years the fitted orbit predicted the sky as well as JPL did; by year six it was 1.6
times worse, 0.18 arcsec from JPL's curve, and the reason is visible in the fit itself: the velocity along the direction a five-year optical arc
constrains least. All four pre-registered predictions held, one of them narrowly. The next lever is not in the dynamics. It is in the weights (the fit
said the assumed half-arcsecond is twice too loose), in the length of the arc, in radar, and in the Yarkovsky drift that JPL carries and we do not.

At 04:30 every morning from now on the task scheduler runs the board again. The prediction ages; the telescopes keep looking; the ledger keeps score.
